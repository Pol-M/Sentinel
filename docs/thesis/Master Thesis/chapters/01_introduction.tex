\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

The name \emph{Sentinel} is derived from the Old Italian word
\emph{sentinella}, referring to a person or thing tasked with watching
over, protecting, and alerting others of potential danger. This is
precisely what \emph{Sentinel} aims to do for a climber: it continuously
watches over the climbing rope, manages it to protect the climber during
the ascent, and reacts immediately -- by braking -- if a fall is 
detected.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

Climbing is a sport and activity that involves moving upward on rock faces or artificial walls using hands and feet for support, strength, and balance. There are several main disciplines: \textbf{bouldering}, which is performed on short walls without ropes; \textbf{sport climbing}, where a rope and pre-placed bolts protect the climber from falls; and \textbf{traditional (trad) climbing}, in which the climber places their own protection as they ascend. In sport and trad climbing, as well as multi-pitch routes, the rope and safety systems are fundamental for fall protection and security.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.63\textwidth]{figures/adam-ondra-day-5-pitch.jpg}
    \caption{Sport climber on rope with belayer below.}
    \label{fig:adam_ondra}
\end{figure}
\vspace{0.4\baselineskip}

In these disciplines, a \emph{belayer} manages the rope from below, feeding or taking in slack as required, and holding the rope in case the climber falls. The belayer is essential for safety: they must always pay close attention, anticipate the climber’s movements, and react instantly to any sudden load on the rope. Managing slack and braking are continuous, active processes.

    \end{subfigure}
    \hfill
    \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.4\textwidth}

        \label{fig:belayer_petzl}
    \end{subfigure}

    \label{fig:belayer_tasks}
\end{figure}

To connect the rope to the anchor and the belayer's harness, a wide
variety of belay devices exists. They differ in how much braking force
they provide on their own and in how much continuous attention the belayer
must still supply. At the simplest end are \textbf{figure-eight descenders},
such as the CAMP Otto~\cite{camp_otto}: the rope is woven through a fixed
aluminium shape and braking relies entirely on rope friction and the
belayer's brake-hand grip, with no mechanical assist when a load suddenly
appears (\cref{fig:camp_otto}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.35\textwidth]{figures/CAMPOtto.jpg}
    \caption{CAMP Otto figure-eight descender.}
    \label{fig:camp_otto}
\end{figure}

A step above are \textbf{manual belay devices}---tube-style designs such
as the Petzl Reverso~\cite{petzl_reverso}. The rope is bent through the
device and, when loaded, is pressed into V-shaped friction grooves that
increase holding force, but catching a fall still depends completely on
the belayer maintaining correct hand position and pulling the brake strand
downward (\cref{fig:petzl_reverso}). These devices are lightweight and
versatile, yet they offer no additional help beyond friction geometry:
slack management and the decision to lock the rope remain fully manual.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.42\textwidth]{figures/PetzlReverso.jpeg}
    \caption{Petzl Reverso manual belay device.}
    \label{fig:petzl_reverso}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Assisted Braking Devices} (ABDs) go further by adding a mechanical
response when the rope is loaded quickly. The belayer must still feed and
take in slack, but the device itself contributes extra holding force during
a fall or hard catch. Within this family, designs differ in whether they
use internal moving parts. \textbf{Geometric ABDs} such as the Mammut
Smart~2.0~\cite{mammut_smart_2} have no moving components: under load, the
rope is forced into a shaped channel and the carabiner orientation
increases friction passively. That geometry assists a skilled belayer but
provides no discrete latch or lever that an actuator could command
(\cref{fig:mammut_smart_2}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.42\textwidth]{figures/MammutSmart2.0.png}
    \caption{Mammut Smart~2.0 geometric assisted-braking device.}
    \label{fig:mammut_smart_2}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Active ABDs} add internal moving parts that react to rope motion or
tension. The Petzl GriGri~\cite{grabcad_petzl_grigri} uses a pivoting cam
that pinches the rope against a grooved sheave when a sudden load is
applied; the Wild Country Revo~\cite{wild_country_revo} uses a centrifugal
wheel that locks when the rope accelerates rapidly in either direction
(\cref{fig:active_abds}). Both reduce belayer effort during a catch, but
their internal layouts differ sharply: the GriGri exposes a single cam
lever with a clear open/closed state, whereas the Revo packs an inertial
wheel, clutch, and locking elements into a compact, tightly coupled
assembly.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.48\textwidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/petzl_grigri_reference.png}
        \caption{Petzl GriGri (cam-assisted).}
        \label{fig:petzl_grigri}
    \end{subfigure}
    \hfill
    \begin{subfigure}[t]{0.48\textwidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/WildCountryRevo.jpg}
        \caption{Wild Country Revo (inertia-assisted).}
        \label{fig:wild_country_revo}
    \end{subfigure}
    \caption{Active assisted-braking devices with internal moving parts.}
    \label{fig:active_abds}
\end{figure}

For Sentinel, the GriGri-style cam architecture is taken as the mechanical
reference. Its pinching mechanism is mechanically straightforward to model,
has a natural motor actuation point on the cam lever, and retains a proven
passive lock-on-load behaviour that can serve as a fail-safe when power is
lost. The Revo's inertial internals would be far harder to motorise and
instrument in a first prototype, and the Smart's geometry-dependent braking
offers no comparable actuation interface. Automating a GriGri-inspired cam
mechanism therefore strikes the best balance between mechanical simplicity,
field-proven safety, and suitability for active motorisation.

\section{Problem Area}
\label{sec:project-area}

However, reliance on a human belayer introduces risks: errors due to fatigue, distraction, or inexperience can result in too much slack or a delayed braking response. Furthermore, climbing requires one person to belay while the other climbs, meaning both cannot climb simultaneously. Automating the belaying process could increase safety, reduce human error, and allow both climbers to be on the wall at once.

This thesis addresses the question: \emph{Can the key safety functions of a human belayer be reliably replaced by an autonomous, mechatronic system?} Such a system would have to actively assess the climber’s state, manage rope slack, detect falls, and operate a braking mechanism in real time, all without human intervention. This challenge requires seamless integration of mechanical engineering (for rope handling and braking), sensors (to monitor movement and tension), and robust embedded software to ensure immediate, safe operation and fail-safe behaviour, all while adhering to strict climbing equipment standards.

If successful, such a device could enable new forms of climbing---such as both partners climbing simultaneously---and greatly reduce the risk posed by belayer error, fatigue, or distraction.

\section{Narrowing}
\label{sec:narrowing}

The problem posed in \cref{sec:project-area} applies in principle to every
roped climbing discipline, environment, and stage of product development.
This thesis does not address that full space. The problem is narrowed to
the following boundary conditions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Climbing context:} sport climbing with a single belay
        from below. Bouldering (no rope) and trad climbing
        (climber-placed protection) lie outside the problem frame.
    \item \textbf{Operating environment:} bench-top rigs and controlled
        indoor tests, not outdoor crags or operational deployment in
        commercial climbing gyms.
    \item \textbf{Rope assumptions:} one standard dynamic rope diameter and
        type, not the full range of ropes or belay configurations on the
        market.
    \item \textbf{Solution maturity:} proof-of-concept level---a laboratory
        demonstrator rather than a manufacturable or certified consumer
        product.
\end{itemize}

\section{Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

\subsection{Principal Objectives}
\label{sec:principal-objectives}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Create a functional prototype of an automated self-belaying
        device.
    \item Perform comprehensive testing and evaluation of the prototype.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Secondary Objectives}
\label{sec:secondary-objectives}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Analyze regulations, standards, and safety requirements
        applicable to such climbing devices.
    \item Survey existing commercial and academic self-belay and
        assisted-braking solutions.
    \item Formulate comprehensive functional and technical requirement
        specifications for the system.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Questions}
\label{sec:research-questions}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item[\textbf{RQ1}] To what extent can an embedded sensing system
        reliably distinguish between a climber pulling in rope during
        normal ascent and the onset of an actual fall?
    \item[\textbf{RQ2}] How can an automated belay mechanism safely
        transition out of a braked state once the climber has resumed a
        valid climbing position, without requiring manual intervention?
    \item[\textbf{RQ3}] Which existing regulations and standards
        applicable to climbing protection equipment are relevant to an
        automated belay device, and what safety requirements do they
        impose on its design?
    \item[\textbf{RQ4}] What mechanical and control-system safeguards are
        necessary to guarantee a safe fallback behaviour (e.g.\ automatic
        rope locking) in the event of a power or electronic failure?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The following are explicitly outside the scope of this thesis, unless
revisited as future work:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Formal certification of the device against climbing safety
        standards (e.g.\ UIAA, CE/EN).
    \item Outdoor field testing with real climbers at height.
    \item Support for multiple rope diameters, types, or conditions
        (e.g.\ wet or icy rope).
    \item Weatherproofing and long-term durability testing of the
        prototype enclosure.
    \item Mass-manufacturability and industrial design for a commercial
        product.
\end{itemize}

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

This thesis contributes the following:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A working proof-of-concept prototype that demonstrated
        automated, sensor-driven belaying with real-time slack management
        and fast emergency braking is technically feasible using
        accessible embedded hardware.
    \item An analysis of existing commercial and academic self-belay
        solutions, situating this project's approach relative to the
        state of the art.
    \item A documented functional and technical requirement
        specification for automated belay devices.
    \item An analysis of applicable regulations, standards, and safety
        requirements relevant to automated belay device design.
    \item Empirical performance data (e.g.\ reaction time, braking
        distance, false-trigger rate) characterising the prototype's
        safety-relevant behaviour under controlled test conditions.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Report Tools}
\label{sec:report-tools}

This section documents the tools used in preparing this report and the
underlying project work.

\subsection{AI Tools}

An AI-assisted coding and documentation environment (Cursor) was used
throughout the project to accelerate firmware scaffolding, documentation
drafting, and LaTeX authoring. All AI-assisted contributions were
reviewed and directed by the author; ambiguous design decisions were
resolved by the author rather than left to automated assumptions, per the
project's development workflow.

\subsection{CAD Tools}

Mechanical design and reference modelling work uses SolidWorks, alongside
publicly available reference CAD models (see \cref{sec:background}) used
for early-stage design inspiration.

\subsection{LaTeX and Documentation Tools}

This report is written in \LaTeX{}, using the community \texttt{ntnuthesis}
document class for the long-form thesis, compiled locally with a MiKTeX
distribution (\texttt{pdflatex}/\texttt{biber}). General project
documentation (hardware specifications, pinouts, and development history)
is maintained in Markdown under \texttt{docs/}.

\subsection{Software Development Environments}

Embedded firmware is developed in C++ using the PlatformIO build system
and toolchain, targeting an embedded microcontroller platform. Version
control is managed with Git.
